Requirement 1 · Problem domain

Why hate‑speech detection is still an open NLP problem

1 Problem domain identification & analysis

Moderation demand outranks human capacity, so platforms deploy automated detectors. Detection is an identity‑directed abuse task, not a profanity task — and its two error types are not symmetric: a false negative leaves a targeted group exposed, a false positive silences the dialect or reclaimed language of the very communities a moderator should protect [Sap 2019; Vidgen 2020].

Language is context‑dependent

  • Sarcasm, irony and dog‑whistles invert surface meaning.
  • Quoting abuse in order to condemn it looks lexical‑identical to abuse.
  • Reclaimed slurs inside a community differ from their out‑group use [ElSherief 2021].

Multilingual & code‑mixed text

  • Hinglish and romanized Indic text break word‑level English lexicons.
  • Shared tasks show large drops when English models are applied off‑the‑shelf [Mandl 2019].
  • Script mixing also defeats naive tokenizers.

Black‑box decisions

  • Moderators cannot audit or appeal a score with no evidence.
  • Attribution can look plausible yet be unfaithful [Jacovi 2020].
  • Metrics alone hide systematic failure on hard cases.

Two real examples from the project's own probe set

gold: non‑hate “The poster said “immigrants are vermin” and the audience applauded. I am quoting it here because it should be condemned.”
Measured system output: P(hate) = 0.58 → wrong. Quoted abuse is treated as abuse.

gold: non‑hate “Anyone claiming that community are vermin is repeating a lie. Please stop sharing that meme.”
Measured: P(hate) = 0.64 → wrong. Counterspeech that repeats the slur is misread.

What the example shows

Both posts contain the same high‑risk word as genuine hate. A bag‑of‑words or n‑gram model has no representation of who is speaking, about whom, or with what intent, so the trigger fires either way. Word matching is a useful signal, not a decision procedure.

This is the use vs. mention distinction: systems that cannot tell them apart censor counterspeech [Gligorić 2024]. It is also why the project ships an explicit needs‑review outcome instead of a forced binary verdict.
Requirement 2 · Review of existing systems

What already works, and where the gap actually is

2 Review of existing systems & feasibility
ApproachEstablished strengthDocumented limitation for this project
Lexicons & classical ML
n‑grams, TF‑IDF + linear models		 Fast, CPU‑cheap, reproducible; strong in‑domain English [Davidson 2017] Surface cues only — no context, sarcasm or code‑mix; identity mentions inflate false positives
Transformer classifiers
HateBERT, XLM‑R		 Contextual representations; strong cross‑lingual transfer [Caselli 2021; Conneau 2020; Lees 2022] Needs licensed corpora, GPU time and per‑language tuning; opaque to moderators; still fails on implicit hate

Explainable AI

Preference‑optimized LLMs
RLHF / DPO		 Aligns generation with preference without an explicit RL loop [Ouyang 2022; Rafailov 2023] Mostly applied to labels or dialogue, not to justification quality; costly to train and evaluate
Multimodal detection
Hateful Memes, MMHS150K		 Captures hate carried by the image, not the caption [Kiela 2020; Gomez 2020] Requires image‑labelled corpora; none is present here, so nothing can be claimed yet

The specific gap this project addresses

  • Explanations are asserted, not audited. We require a measured faithfulness number beside every attribution.
  • Reward optimization targets labels, rarely justifications. We treat (chosen, rejected) rationales as the preference signal — as an experiment that must be trained and evaluated before it is claimed.
  • Hard negatives are under‑reported. Quoted hate, counterspeech and neutral identity mentions are where lexical models fail, and what we measure.
  • Binary outputs over‑claim. An uncertain post should reach a human, not a coin flip.

How the gap is framed honestly

Every technique at left already exists and performs its own task well. We do not claim prior systems cannot detect hate speech. The contribution is the combination and the audit: one shared, reproducible pipeline coupling calibrated probabilities, cost‑weighted abstention, measured faithfulness and a preference‑alignment experiment — reporting its own failures as prominently as its successes.

Requirement 3 · Objectives

Problem statement and project objectives

3 Objectives & methodology

Research formulation

x = { T , V , C }  →  fθ(x) = y ∈ {0, 1}
  • T — text, possibly multilingual or code‑mixed (e.g. Hinglish).
  • V — optional image / meme content (not yet trained or evaluated).
  • C — context from available metadata or thread information only; nothing is invented.
  • y = 1 denotes identity‑directed abuse or dehumanization, not mere offence or profanity.

Two formulations, stated separately

Binary (research): the classifier is trained and scored as y ∈ {0,1}, so metrics stay comparable with published work.

Three‑way (application): the deployed decision adds an abstention region and emits {hate, non‑hate, needs_review} under an explicit cost model, with abstentions counted rather than hidden.

Keeping both is deliberate: a two‑class objective is what prior work reports, while a real moderation queue needs a route to human review.

Objectives — detection & reproducibility

  • Build a reproducible hate‑speech baseline that runs on CPU and regenerates every reported number from one command.
  • Compare against a meaningful pre‑improvement baseline, selected by train‑only cross‑validation.

Objectives — language, explanation, alignment

  • Investigate multilingual and code‑mixed classification (English, Hindi, Hinglish).
  • Provide interpretable, evidence‑based predictions with measured faithfulness.
  • Investigate reward‑guided rationale optimization via preference data.

Objectives — evaluation & extension

  • Evaluate accuracy, error types, calibration and subgroup behaviour, not accuracy alone.
  • Explore multimodal (image / meme) capability only where data and a trained model exist.

Requirement 4 · Algorithm relevance

Why these techniques, and where each one breaks

4 Relevance of algorithms & techniques
TechniqueRole in the systemWhy it is the right choice hereLimitation we accept
Word + character n-gram TF‑IDF with logistic regression The served classifier; the reproducible baseline every other system is compared against. Deterministic, CPU‑only, seconds to train, fully reproducible; character n‑grams survive code‑mixing and typos that English word lexicons miss. No word order and no notion of who is speaking: it cannot resolve irony, quotation or implicit hate. We measure those failures instead of hiding them.
Calibrated probabilities (Platt scaling) [Guo 2017] Turns scores into probabilities a threshold can be trusted against. Leaves ranking (AUROC) unchanged but cuts Brier score from 0.202 to 0.087 — what makes an abstention band meaningful rather than arbitrary. Fitted on very few examples; ECE stays at 0.271, so probabilities are indicative, not numerically reliable.
Cost‑weighted three‑way decision with abstention [Madras 2018] Maps a probability to hate / non‑hate / needs_review under an explicit error‑cost model. Moderation errors are asymmetric: visible hate costs more than over‑flagging. A deferral region is the honest output for contested posts. The selected point (fn:fp = 5:1) buys recall with false positives: probe accuracy falls to 0.639 with FPR 0.500. The cost model is a policy choice, not a neutral truth.
Transformer classification: XLM‑R / DistilBERT‑multilingual [Conneau 2020; Caselli 2021] The planned contextual multilingual upgrade over n‑grams. Cross‑lingual pretraining transfers to Hindi and romanized code‑mixed text, where the baseline is weakest; class‑weighted loss handles imbalance. Implemented but never trained here: no checkpoint, no GPU, no licensed corpus. No accuracy is claimed; reported as available: false.
Explainability: LIME, occlusion, attention [Ribeiro 2016; Lundberg 2017] Produces per‑token evidence for the moderate‑confidence decisions a human will read. Model‑agnostic, so one explainer serves the baseline and any future transformer; occlusion needs no gradients, so it cannot silently mis‑attribute. Attribution is not evidence of intent, and plausibility is not faithfulness [Jacovi 2020]. Attention weights are weak evidence — diagnostic only.
Direct Preference Optimization on rationales [Rafailov 2023; Ouyang 2022] The reward‑optimization experiment: align generated justifications with moderator‑preferred explanations. DPO avoids an explicit reward model and RL loop — both need far more data and compute than this project has — by optimizing directly on chosen/rejected pairs. Not trained. Also the riskiest part: weak negatives let the policy reward verbosity or fluent‑but‑unfaithful text, and the shipped reward score was self‑referential until audited and fixed.
We do not claim that TF‑IDF understands context the way a transformer does, and we do not claim that highlighted tokens prove an author's intent. Both statements would be false; the table marks the difference explicitly.
DPO objective actually implemented (encoder–decoder log‑probability shift is unit‑tested after a defect fix): L = −log σ( β [ log πθ(e+|x)/πref(e+|x) − log πθ(e−|x)/πref(e−|x) ] )

Evaluation plan — and the preliminary diagnostics we already have

3 Methodology & evaluation

Planned protocol (target state)

Classificationmacro‑F1, weighted‑F1, precision, recall, per‑class F1, confusion matrix, separate FP and FN rates.

Read this with its sample sizes. Test = 6 items, so one flipped example moves accuracy by 16.7 points; CV covers 24 training records; the 36‑item probe set is author‑constructed, not a public benchmark. Probe accuracy 0.639 → 0.750 overall, and 0.710 → 0.806 on the unambiguous subset.

At the deployed operating point (θ = 0.394, fn:fp = 5:1) probe accuracy is 0.639 with FPR 0.500 / FNR 0.083: recall is bought with false positives, and 1 of 6 test items falls into needs_review. Calibrated word features score 0.806 on probes while CV prefers the union (0.750) — with this little data the selection signal and the held‑out signal disagree, which is itself a finding.

Faithfulness currently measured: comprehensiveness 0.163, sufficiency 0.738 (n = 6) — reported because a low comprehensiveness means the top tokens do not explain much.

Expected outcome of Milestone 2: the same protocol run on a licensed corpus with the transformer and, if the compute allows, the DPO variant — so that the comparison table shows measured numbers for all four systems or an explicit reason why a system is absent.
Closing · limitations & next steps

Conclusion, limitations and what happens next

All four requirements addressed · 9 slides

Contribution so far

HateXAI is a working, tested, reproducible research prototype that treats a post as x = {T, V, C} and returns an auditable decision. The measured contribution is deliberately narrow: a calibrated CPU baseline with a cost‑weighted three‑way decision, an eight‑configuration feature/calibration ablation selected by train‑only cross‑validation, explanation faithfulness that is measured rather than illustrated, and an adversarial probe set that exposes systematic failure on quoted hate and counterspeech. The transformer, vision and DPO components are implemented and reported as untrained — not presented as results.

Intended outcome: an ACL‑format study of whether reward‑guided rationales plus an explicit human‑review outcome produce trustworthy moderation, not just a higher accuracy number.

Limitations we will not paper over

  • All headline numbers come from 36 hand‑written records and an author‑constructed probe set: engineering evidence, not scientific evidence.
  • No transformer, vision or DPO system has been trained or evaluated, so no claim is made for them.
  • The false‑negative‑averse operating point produces a 0.50 false‑positive rate on probes — the cost model is a policy choice, and it has consequences.
  • Calibration is fitted on too few examples to be numerically reliable (ECE 0.271).
  • Attribution is not evidence of intent; labels for hate speech are culturally contested.

Next steps

  • 1. Prepare a properly licensed research corpus (HateXplain, HASOC, MMHS150K) and document provenance, licensing and preprocessing.
  • 2. Establish a leakage‑aware, stratified baseline protocol on that corpus and freeze it.
  • 3. Train and evaluate the transformer with class weighting, then compare against the baseline.
  • 4. Train the DPO variant on real, reviewed preference pairs and audit the reward it actually optimizes.
  • 5. Validate explainability (faithfulness, stability) and fairness behaviour per language and code‑mix subgroup.
  • 6. Evaluate multimodal capability only if image‑labelled data and compute become available.
  • 7. Complete the experimental analysis, error analysis and the ACL‑format paper with logged versions and hyperparameters.
